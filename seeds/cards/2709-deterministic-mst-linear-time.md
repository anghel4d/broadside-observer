# A Deterministic Linear Time Algorithm for Minimum Spanning Tree

## One-sentence takeaway

A deterministic comparison-based algorithm computes a minimum spanning tree in O(m) time, the first improvement since Chazelle's O(m α(m,n)) bound in 2000, by showing the comparison complexity is linear and invoking the Pettie–Ramachandran reduction.

## Why it's lovely

This closes one of the oldest open problems in algorithms: Borůvka's electrification-of-Moravia problem from the 1920s has had randomized linear time since Karger–Klein–Tarjan, and now has deterministic linear time with edges treated as opaque comparable keys. The route is a clean idea: a certificate procedure that cheaply finds a forest which "supports" most edges (each supported edge is the heaviest on some cycle, so it cannot be in the MST), leaving a recursive instance at most half the size, so T(m) = T(m/2) + O(m). The authors' AI acknowledgment says the main ideas were generated by an OpenAI system with minimal human intellectual intervention and then verified and simplified by the authors, which makes it a datapoint for the Broadside thread on what agents can now do in theory work. For Anoptic the bridge is small but real: MST sits under clustering, mesh and navigation-graph tools, and the paper is a reminder that a textbook primitive can still have headroom.

## Key ideas

- **Result.** Deterministic linear time for MST in the comparison model; the paper shows the deterministic comparison complexity T*(m,n) = O(m) and then applies the Pettie–Ramachandran (JACM 2002) reduction.
- **Edge filtering.** A forest F supports edge e if e's endpoints are joined by F using weights at most w(e); supported edges are not in the MST unless they are in F, so only F plus the unsupported edges survive to recurse.
- **Certificate procedure.** Vertices are repeatedly projected onto portals through low-weight spokes, with a deterministic schedule of find and project actions over t^2 rounds, so that edges are never re-queried too often.
- **Recursion.** A constant number of Borůvka phases shrinks the vertex count enough that the recursive calls total at most m/2 edges, giving T(m) = T(m/2) + O(m).
- **Provenance.** Section 1.4 describes an AI-generated core argument: a team of 15 agents first proved an o(m α) bound after 15 hours, then repeated prompting tightened it step by step, followed by human verification and simplification.

## Caveats

I read the abstract, the introduction, the technical overview and the AI acknowledgments of v1 (23 Nov 2026, 30 pp.); I did not check the proofs or the certificate procedure in detail. It is a one-day-old unrefereed preprint. The result is in the comparison model, so it does not by itself say anything about practical running time, where randomized or simple algorithms will still win for ordinary graphs. The AI-generation claim comes from the authors' own statement.
